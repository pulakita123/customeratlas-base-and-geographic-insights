# CustomerAtlas: Customer Base & Geographic Insights — Project Report

## 1. Introduction
For this project, I used Excel to explore a customer dataset and build a dashboard around customer growth, tenure and distribution. I wanted the report to answer more useful questions than simply showing how many records were in a file.

## 2. The business question
I approached the work from a business reporting perspective. A company may have a large customer list, but it also needs to understand how that customer base has changed over time, how long customers have been associated with it, and whether its customer base is concentrated in a few countries or companies.

The main question I worked towards was: how can I turn customer-level information into a clear view that helps someone understand the shape and development of the customer base?

## 3. Preparing the data
The project includes an original CSV and a cleaned CSV. The preparation activities described in the project include removing duplicate or invalid records, standardising country and company names, and creating fields such as subscription year and tenure groupings.

I treated data preparation as an important part of the analysis rather than jumping straight into charts. If categories are inconsistent or customer records are duplicated, the dashboard can communicate misleading totals.

## 4. Analysing customer patterns
I used PivotTables to summarise customers by year, country, company and tenure. This made it possible to compare segments and bring the main measures into a dashboard. Slicers for year, country and company allow the report to be explored interactively.

The business questions focused on customer count, acquisition patterns, average tenure and the distribution of customers across geography and companies.

## 5. What the analysis suggests
The original project notes describe geographic and company-level concentration, a meaningful longer-tenure segment and variation in customer additions across subscription years.

I see these as useful starting points for further analysis. Concentration may warrant closer monitoring, tenure can help identify groups for deeper retention analysis, and acquisition patterns can point to periods worth investigating.

I would not treat these patterns as proof of customer loyalty, profitability or the cause of growth. Those conclusions require additional information.

## 6. Communicating the results
I used an Excel dashboard to bring key customer measures and breakdowns into one place. KPI cards provide a quick overview, while charts and PivotTables show how customers are distributed. Slicers make it possible to explore the report by year, country and company.

## 7. What I learned
This project helped me practise cleaning categorical data, creating derived fields, using PivotTables to answer business questions and designing a dashboard for interactive exploration.

It also reinforced an important point: a dashboard is only as reliable as its definitions. Customer count, tenure and subscription year need clear rules so that users understand what each number represents.

## 8. Limitations and next steps
The project is descriptive and does not include revenue, cost, churn events, customer satisfaction, contract value or transaction history. I would next validate every displayed metric against the source data, document the exact field definitions and extend the analysis with additional business data if available.

## 9. Conclusion
I approached this project as a customer-base analysis exercise: prepare the data, define useful measures, explore patterns across time and segments, and communicate the results through an interactive Excel dashboard. It demonstrates my ability to turn a dataset into structured business reporting while recognising the limits of what the data can prove.
